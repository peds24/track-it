Track It · v1 interface specification

A list you can read in one glance

Track It records what you are part-way through — shows, movies, books, comics, manga — and gets out of the way. This is the visual system: what a row looks like, what carries colour, and what we deliberately leave out.

Position

The app is opened, tapped once, and closed. That is the entire interaction, most days. Every decision below protects it: nothing may compete with the row you came to advance.

The reference point is Things — not its blue, its restraint: generous spacing, one weight of emphasis, and a willingness to leave a screen almost empty. Track It goes further in one respect. It carries no artwork at all.

This pass pushes that restraint toward a second reference: dedicated e-ink displays — thermostats, hotel room panels, remote controls. Nothing on those devices is trying to look friendly. Every control is a bordered rectangle, every number is set heavy enough to read from across a room, and colour, where it exists at all, is reserved for the one thing that needs to interrupt you. None of that contradicts the principles below — it is what "text is the artwork" looks like taken further: ornament comes from making the necessary elements assertive, not from adding new ones. Colour stays for now, deepened rather than removed; whether it survives the next pass is still open.

Principles

  • Text is the artwork

    No covers, no thumbnails, no placeholder tiles. A title set well at 17pt identifies a book faster than a 44px cover, and never shows a grey box when the artwork is missing.

  • The medium is a word, not a colour

    Every row is labelled MANGA, BOOK, SHOW. Five media types in five colours would be a rainbow fighting the restraint — and the word is already unambiguous.

  • One hue, shared by every medium

    Cobalt marks the kind label and the progress bar — what a thing is, and how far in you are. Every medium uses the same blue, so the colour identifies a class of information rather than sorting rows into five competing groups. Both appearances sit in the text column, so a row reads as one object.

  • Nothing is decorated twice

    A row is separated by a hairline or by space — never both. State is shown by one signal, never by colour and weight and a badge together.

  • Controls are bordered; lists are not

    A hairline still separates one row from the next — a list is read, not operated. Anything you can press draws a full-weight ink border instead, the way a button on a physical panel is a rectangle you can see before you touch it.

Palette

Every neutral got darker or lighter this pass — ink to near-black, muted to a shade that no longer needs squinting, rules split into two weights. Nothing changed hue. The list should look like it is printed, not projected.

Second value is the dark-theme counterpart. Ink stopped short of true black on white — #0A0B0D reads as ink, not as a rendering bug — but it is as close as body text gets before it starts to look like a design choice rather than a print. Rule, strong is new: hairlines still separate rows at 1px, but every bordered control — a button, a chip, a field — now draws its border from ink itself, at 1.5px. A list is quiet; a control is not, and the two should not share a border weight.

Pause and danger are also new to this document — they existed in the app before they existed here. Both deepen the same way the accent did: less pastel, more ink, so a swipe action reads as decisive rather than tinted.

Type

One family: the platform system face — SF Pro on iOS, Roboto on Android. This is a decision, not a default. It is the face the OS renders most sharply at 13pt, it ships with Dynamic Type support we get for free, and a media tracker has no reason to introduce a voice of its own on top of the titles it is listing.

Rows are separated by a hairline, not a card. Cards would add a border, a radius, a shadow and a gap to say what a 1px rule already says, and they push the list density down by roughly a third — two fewer rows visible on a 390pt screen.

  • The title owns its line Full width to truncate into, and no second element competing with the one thing you are scanning for.
  • Kind label leads the meta line Set in accent, in a fixed first position, so the eye learns one place and one colour for "what is this".
  • The count sits directly above its bar Number and measure occupy the same block, so "4 of 34" and the width that depicts it are read together.
  • Standalone books have no bar Nothing to be part-way through numerically, so no progress is drawn — absence is the signal.
  • One control, one word "Done" works for an episode and a volume alike. It fills with accent only while held.
  • The control is the only bordered rectangle in the row Everything else is separated by space or a hairline. A full-weight ink border reads as "you can press this" precisely because nothing else on the row uses one.

What we don't do

  • No

    Cover art or thumbnails. The v1 catalogue is hand-typed, so most covers would be missing anyway — and a grid of grey placeholders is a worse first impression than a clean list.

  • No

    A different colour per medium. Kind labels are coloured, but all five share one accent. Five hues would consume the whole palette to restate a word already on screen, and would make a mixed list look sorted into groups that mean nothing.

  • No

    Icons in rows. A glyph for "manga" is a guess the reader has to decode. The word is not.

  • No

    Ratings, streaks or statistics. They turn a record of what you are reading into a scoreboard about it.

  • No

    A Done tab. Finished things are reachable behind one chip, not given equal billing with what you are actually doing.

  • Not yet

    Export, import, or any Settings screen. Deferred past v1, which leaves the app with two tabs and — worth stating plainly — no backup path at all. The library lives in one place on one phone.
