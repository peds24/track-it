Track It · v1 interface specification

A list you can read in one glance

Track It records what you are part-way through — shows, movies, books, comics, manga — and gets out of the way. This is the visual system: what a row looks like, what carries colour, and what we deliberately leave out.

Position

The app is opened, tapped once, and closed. That is the entire interaction, most days. Every decision below protects it: nothing may compete with the row you came to advance.

The reference point is Things — not its blue, its restraint: generous spacing, one weight of emphasis, and a willingness to leave a screen almost empty. Track It goes further in one respect. It carries no artwork at all.

This pass pushes that restraint toward a second reference: dedicated e-ink displays — thermostats, hotel room panels, remote controls. Nothing on those devices is trying to look friendly. Every control is a bordered rectangle, every number is set heavy enough to read from across a room, and colour, where it exists at all, is reserved for the one thing that needs to interrupt you. None of that contradicts the principles below — it is what "text is the artwork" looks like taken further: ornament comes from making the necessary elements assertive, not from adding new ones. Light mode's ground took the reference literally, too — the grey-green of the panel itself, not a software white standing in for paper.

Colour did not survive the pass after this one. On a tinted ground built to look like a physical material, a software blue or orange sitting on top of it read as exactly that — software, dropped onto something that was supposed to be a surface. Underline replaces it as the one mark reserved for what needs to stand out, and where two states still need to differ in how serious they are, weight now carries that instead of hue.

Principles

  • Text is the artwork

    No covers, no thumbnails, no placeholder tiles. A title set well at 17pt identifies a book faster than a 44px cover, and never shows a grey box when the artwork is missing.

  • The medium is a word, not a colour

    Every row is labelled MANGA, BOOK, SHOW. Five media types in five colours would be a rainbow fighting the restraint — and the word is already unambiguous.

  • One mark, shared by every medium

    An underline marks the kind label; ink alone fills the progress bar — what a thing is, and how far in you are. Every medium gets the same treatment, so the mark identifies a class of information rather than sorting rows into five competing groups. Both appearances sit in the text column, so a row reads as one object.

  • Nothing is decorated twice

    A row is separated by a hairline or by space — never both. State is shown by one signal, never by colour and weight and a badge together.

  • Controls are bordered; lists are not

    A hairline still separates one row from the next — a list is read, not operated. Anything you can press draws a full-weight ink border instead, the way a button on a physical panel is a rectangle you can see before you touch it.

Palette

Light mode now takes its ground from an actual e-ink panel rather than from paper-white software convention — a grey with a cast of green in it, the colour of the film itself rather than of ink on top of it. Dark mode does not chase the same authenticity: e-ink is a reflective, light-only technology, so there is no real panel to match, and a green-tinted dark mode would read as an odd choice rather than a considered one. It stays a plain, properly dark theme.

Second value is the dark-theme counterpart. Muted and faint moved off the neutral blue-grey family and picked up the same green cast as the ground, at a fraction of its saturation — grey ink on tinted paper, not a cooler grey sitting on top of a warmer one. Rule, strong is new: hairlines still separate rows at 1px, but every bordered control — a button, a chip, a field — now draws its border from ink itself, at 1.5px. A list is quiet; a control is not, and the two should not share a border weight.

There is no seventh swatch. Accent, pause and danger are gone — every one of them read strange sitting on a tinted ground that was never designed to hold a hue, cobalt or burnt orange both landing as a mismatched software colour dropped onto a physical material. This is now a two-value palette: ink, and everything between ink and ground.

What colour used to signal, underline signals now — the kind label, a header's action, the active tab, a "no" in the list below. One mark, doing the job one hue used to, and it costs nothing when the ground itself has no fixed colour to be "on" — which a real e-ink panel, lit by whatever is in the room, effectively does not. The two states that still need to differ in how much they matter — delete versus pause, on a swipe — now differ in actual weight: pause stays as light as the row behind it, delete inverts to a solid block of ink. See the row and swipe-action examples below.

Type

Still one family, still the platform's own — but its monospace register now: SF Mono on iOS, Roboto Mono on Android, falling back to whatever fixed-width face the OS ships. Every character sits in an equal box, the way it does on the devices this pass is chasing — a thermostat's readout, a terminal, a label typed rather than set. It is sharper at small sizes for the same reason monospace code is: no two glyphs share a curve built to look good next to different neighbours, so there is less for a low-refresh panel to smear.

The weight scale collapsed to match — a system monospace face rarely ships the six-step gradient a variable proportional face does. Three stops now do all the work: regular, medium and bold. Negative tracking is gone too; a proportional display face tightens to close up its own curves, but a monospace face is already an even grid, and tightening it just crowds the boxes.

Rows are separated by a hairline, not a card. Cards would add a border, a radius, a shadow and a gap to say what a 1px rule already says, and they push the list density down by roughly a third — two fewer rows visible on a 390pt screen.

  • The title owns its line Full width to truncate into, and no second element competing with the one thing you are scanning for.
  • Kind label leads the meta line Underlined, in a fixed first position, so the eye learns one place and one mark for "what is this".
  • The count sits directly above its bar Number and measure occupy the same block, so "4 of 34" and the width that depicts it are read together.
  • Standalone books have no bar Nothing to be part-way through numerically, so no progress is drawn — absence is the signal.
  • One control, one word "Done" works for an episode and a volume alike. Pressed, it inverts to solid ink rather than filling with a colour that no longer exists.
  • The control is the only bordered rectangle in the row Everything else is separated by space or a hairline. A full-weight ink border reads as "you can press this" precisely because nothing else on the row uses one.

What we don't do

  • No

    Cover art or thumbnails. The v1 catalogue is hand-typed, so most covers would be missing anyway — and a grid of grey placeholders is a worse first impression than a clean list.

  • No

    Colour, anywhere. The kind label used to carry a shared accent; it and every other coloured mark are underlined or bolded now instead. Five hues per medium would have consumed the whole palette to restate a word already on screen — one shared mark does the same job without needing a palette at all.

  • No

    Icons in rows. A glyph for "manga" is a guess the reader has to decode. The word is not.

  • No

    Ratings, streaks or statistics. They turn a record of what you are reading into a scoreboard about it.

  • Not yet

    Export, import, or any Settings screen. Deferred past v1, which leaves the app with three tabs and — worth stating plainly — no backup path at all. The library lives in one place on one phone.
